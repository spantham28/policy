Accelerating Building Electrification in New England: A Legislative Roadmap

Team
Technology Policy research team, Harvard IOP Policy Program
Published
Fall 2023
Type
Policy brief, open access
DOI
10.xxxx/IOP.TECH.2023.002 (registration pending)

Abstract

Buildings account for approximately 27% of greenhouse gas emissions in New England — among the highest regional shares in the United States — driven largely by natural gas heating in aging residential stock. This brief examines the policy, economic, and infrastructure barriers to rapid residential building electrification across the six New England states, with particular focus on Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Maine.

We evaluate the current legislative landscape, identify coordination failures between state utility commissions and building codes, and assess the adequacy of existing federal incentives (including IRA heat pump rebates) for low- and moderate-income households. Our central recommendation is a New England Regional Electrification Compact — a coordinated, multi-state framework that aligns building codes, utility incentive programs, workforce training, and grid modernization investment around a shared 2035 electrification milestone.

Background

The residential sector presents both the greatest decarbonization challenge and the greatest opportunity in New England. Over 60% of the region's housing stock was built before 1980, with heating systems dependent on oil, propane, or natural gas. Replacing these systems with electric heat pumps — even at current grid emissions intensities — reduces lifecycle carbon emissions by 40–70% in most New England states.

Despite strong consumer incentives under the Inflation Reduction Act, heat pump adoption has lagged projections. Interviews with utility program managers and building contractors identified three persistent barriers: (1) upfront cost burden for households not qualifying for means-tested rebates, (2) a shortage of certified heat pump installers, and (3) inadequate grid infrastructure in lower-density service territories. These barriers are fundamentally regional coordination problems that no single state can solve in isolation.

Policy recommendations

  1. Establish a New England Regional Electrification Compact.We recommend the six New England governors authorize a regional electrification compact — modeled on the Transportation Climate Initiative — setting a shared 2035 milestone for all new residential heating installations to be electric-ready, with harmonized building code standards.
  2. Bridge the Installer Workforce Gap.We propose a regional registered apprenticeship program, co-funded by state workforce development agencies and utility ratepayer funds, targeting 10,000 new certified heat pump technicians by 2028. Program design should prioritize veterans and workers transitioning from fossil fuel trades.
  3. Expand Utility On-Bill Financing.We recommend state utility commissions mandate on-bill financing programs allowing households to finance heat pump installations at zero upfront cost, with repayment tied to utility savings. Connecticut's existing program provides a proven template for regional replication.
  4. Accelerate Distribution Grid Upgrades.We recommend New England states coordinate through ISO-NE to prioritize distribution grid investments in low-density service territories where heat pump load growth would otherwise trigger costly grid reinforcement, using a shared cost-allocation framework.
